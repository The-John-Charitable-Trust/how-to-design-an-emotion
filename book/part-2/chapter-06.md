# Chapter 6: The Blueprint of Mind

*The Theory of Constrained Identity*

[Notes](../references/notes/chapter-06.md) · [Bibliography](../references/bibliography/chapter-06.md)

In chapter 5, I ended the false dichotomy between structure and variation by assigning classical and prototype theories different explanatory responsibilities. Classical theory specifies identity conditions. Prototype theory explains categorization over admissible instances.

Rather than treating prototype theory as an account of conceptual identity, I recast it as a theory of categorization operating downstream from conceptual identity. Chapter 6 builds the architecture that makes this division of explanatory labor possible.

A concept cannot survive when theorists relocate its identity conditions downstream into similarity, use, prediction, habit, culture, or performance. Those phenomena matter. They explain how rational agents and computational systems instantiate concepts, categorize admissible instances, evaluate performance, learn from experience, and optimize execution. Each presupposes the concept’s intensional structure rather than accounting for it.

This chapter gives the concept its computational architecture.

I propose the Theory of Constrained Identity. The model separates four functions that theory too often conflates: conceptual identity, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation.

Once I distinguish these functions, I no longer have to choose between structure and variability because each occupies a different level of the architecture.

The constraint layer specifies identity conditions and admissibility. The instantiation layer realizes those conditions under determinate values. The categorization layer organizes admissible instances according to similarity, typicality, contextual relevance, and graded membership without redefining conceptual identity. The goal layer regulates those categorized instances relative to governing purposes.

This is the key: identity conditions do not perform every explanatory task. They do not explain similarity, typicality, usefulness, priority, error correction, learning, or optimization by themselves. They define the admissible space within which those downstream processes operate.

Similarity belongs downstream. Categorization belongs downstream. Learning belongs downstream. Optimization belongs downstream. Prototype effects belong downstream. Similarity, categorization, prototype effects, learning, and optimization all contribute to computational execution, by which I mean the operational application of conceptual identity to admissible instances. None of these downstream processes, however, generates the identity conditions they presuppose.

The model therefore rests on five computational invariants. Identity conditions remain stable unless the constraint structure changes. Every admissible instance satisfies those identity conditions before categorization begins. Categorization organizes admissible instances without redefining conceptual identity. Goals do not determine the admissibility of instances, but they do evaluate whether a constraint structure is a good one to have. Learning and optimization refine computational execution without altering the identity conditions they presuppose. Consequently, no downstream success condition alone constitutes a concept.

The result is a computational architecture that preserves stable identity conditions while permitting flexible execution. Those identity conditions authorize meaningful variation, and downstream computational processes realize rather than replace conceptual identity.

This chapter completes the architecture of conceptual identity. But architecture alone does not yet explain meaning. Identity conditions determine what may count as an admissible instance. They do not yet explain how concepts come to stand for anything beyond themselves.

That is the task of Chapter 7, The Constructivist Semiotics: The Architecture of Meaning. There I explain how constraint structures acquire semantic significance, how constructs come to stand for their referents, and how interpretation binds conceptual identity to meaning.

I separate four architectural functions so that each performs a distinct explanatory role: conceptual identity, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation. Computational execution operates over those functions through similarity computation, evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization. A concept supplies identity conditions. Instantiation realizes those conditions under determinate values. Prototype-guided categorization organizes admissible instances according to similarity, typicality, contextual relevance, and graded membership. Goal-directed evaluation regulates those categorized instances. When theory collapses these functions, it mistakes variation for identity, performance for meaning, and categorization for definition.

In this chapter, I therefore move from critique to specification. I no longer ask why existing theories fail to preserve identity conditions. I now state the architecture that preserves them.

I call this model computational because it distinguishes architectural functions from the computational processes that operate over them. In Chapter 4, I argued that many contemporary theories orphan identity conditions by relocating them downstream into categorization, prediction, learning, and performance. In Appendix C, Reconstructing Goal-Based Concepts, I showed that goals regulate the application of concepts without generating the identity conditions that make those concepts possible. The Theory of Constrained Identity generalizes that reconstruction into a layered computational architecture. Identity conditions specify admissibility. Instantiation realizes admissible instances. Categorization organizes those instances. Goal-directed evaluation regulates them. Computational execution then applies similarity computation, evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization without redefining conceptual identity.

I begin with the first and governing layer: the constraint layer. Before an agent uses a concept, learns it, categorizes by it, or evaluates through it, the concept must have conditions of identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-1">1</a></sup>

A concept is an intensional constraint structure. It specifies the internal rule structure that determines what may count as a valid instance. It is not a list of observed cases. It is not a region of similarity. It is not a statistical summary. It is the set of structural requirements that governs permissible combinations of properties and relations.,,

Identity conditions fix conceptual sameness. They determine what makes a concept the same concept across time, agents, and contexts. They do not specify every particular value an instance may take. Instead, they specify the permissible relations within which those values may count as instances at all.

Admissibility follows from identity conditions. A candidate instance counts as admissible only if it satisfies the constraints the concept specifies. Before an agent sorts, ranks, evaluates, or uses an instance, the instance must first belong to the admissible space defined by those identity conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-2">2</a></sup>

An instance is a determinate realization of a concept within that admissible space. It assigns values, resolves variables, and places the concept under concrete conditions. Variation among instances reflects execution rather than identity. Differences in magnitude, configuration, embodiment, context, or implementation therefore represent different realizations of the same concept rather than different concepts. Environmental input, bodily state, and physiological process may shape execution, but they do not supply conceptual identity.,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-3">3</a></sup>

This distinction explains why concepts are non-extensional. A concept is not identical to the set of its instances. Instances may multiply, disappear, cluster, scatter, or vary in distribution without changing the concept. Extension depends on what occurs across contexts. Intension governs what may count as an occurrence in the first place. The actual never exhausts the possible.

The distinction between concepts and categories is equally important. A category groups admissible instances relative to purposes or norms. It may track typicality, frequency, usefulness, danger, elegance, efficiency, or practical relevance. But conceptual identity does not rise and fall with those groupings. A shift in category boundaries does not, by itself, revise the concept.

A simple illustration clarifies the point. Suppose a concept requires a defined structural relation among elements. Different instances may realize that relation under different values, magnitudes, or contexts. As long as the governing relation remains intact, the identity conditions remain stable.

The constraint layer therefore fixes identity conditions, determines admissibility, distinguishes concepts from their instances and categories, and resists extensional reduction. It provides the stable architecture within which downstream instantiation, categorization, evaluation, learning, and optimization may occur.

Having established the constraint layer, I now turn to the first downstream layer: instantiation. The constraint layer fixes identity conditions; the instantiation layer realizes those conditions under determinate values. Instantiation gives admissible concepts concrete realization, but realization alone does not determine how those realizations will be interpreted, organized, or evaluated. Categorization organizes those realizations next. The goal layer comes last. Goals specify the criteria by which agents evaluate categorized instances. They do not specify identity conditions. They regulate the use of instances that already satisfy them.,

I locate variation at the level of goal selection, prioritization, and contextual application. Different domains, communities, institutions, or systems may apply different regulatory criteria to the same concept and its admissible instances. They may value different features, impose different thresholds, adopt different standards of performance, or use context to determine which constraint structure a lexical term invokes. Those differences generate different evaluations without multiplying concepts because they regulate the application of identity conditions rather than redefining them. Identity conditions remain stable unless agents revise the admissibility conditions themselves. When agents change goals, thresholds, priorities, or contextual selections, they change evaluation rather than conceptual identity.,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-4">4</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-5">5</a></sup>

If a term like right indexes a moral claim in one context and a legal entitlement in another, the shift does not prove that the concept is fuzzy or constructed on the fly. It proves that the same linguistic pointer can access different stable architectures. Each architecture maintains its own identity conditions.

The rule is simple: the constraint structure fixes identity conditions. If the constraint changes, the concept changes. If the constraint remains and only the realization varies, identity remains intact. What appears to be conceptual change often reflects modifications to instantiation, categorization, or goal evaluation rather than to the identity conditions themselves.

**A worked case: anger.**

Abstraction hides its weaknesses. So I now apply the Theory of Constrained Identity to a single concept in a single system.

The system is specified. It is the folk-psychological concept of anger as used by adult speakers of contemporary American English in ordinary interpersonal life. It is not anger in every culture. It is not a scientific kind. I fix the system first because constraint without universality requires it. Identity conditions hold within a system. They need not hold across all of them.

**The constraint layer.** Within this system, an episode is admissible as anger only if it satisfies four conditions.

First, appraised offense. The agent construes some event as a wrong, slight, or obstruction attributable to an agent, including oneself.

Second, other-directed evaluation. The agent holds that agent accountable. The construal is not merely "this is bad." It is "you did this, and you should not have."

Third, an affective realization. The episode is realized in core affect, typically unpleasant and aroused. I state this as a constraint on admissible values, not a fixed value. Arousal may be high or low; valence must be negative relative to the offense.

Fourth, an action orientation. The episode includes a readiness to confront, correct, retaliate, or restore, even if the agent suppresses it.

These are admissibility conditions. They do not describe a neural signature. They do not require a facial expression. They state what an episode must satisfy to count as anger in this system.

**The instantiation layer.** Instances realize those conditions under determinate values. Road rage realizes them with high arousal and an urge to shout. Cold resentment at a colleague realizes them with low arousal and a plan to withhold cooperation. Anger at oneself after a missed deadline realizes them with the self as the accountable agent. These episodes differ in physiology, behavior, and feeling. Each satisfies the same constraint structure. Variation lives in the values. Identity lives in the constraints.

**The categorization layer.** Only admitted instances are grouped. Here prototype effects do their proper work. In this system, a shouted confrontation over an obvious insult is a highly typical instance. Quiet resentment is less typical. Speakers rate the first as "more anger-like." That rating is real and measurable. It is a fact about the distribution of admitted instances, not about the concept's identity. Typicality ranks members. It does not admit them.

**The goal layer.** Goals regulate how admitted instances are used. A therapist's goal may be regulation, so she groups anger episodes by intensity and triggers. A lawyer's goal may be culpability, so she groups them by provocation and control. A parent's goal may be teaching, so she groups them by appropriateness. Each goal produces a different grouping. None of them changes what counts as anger.

**Misapplication.** The model now explains error. Suppose a man reports that he is angry at the weather for ruining his picnic. If he truly construes no agent as accountable, the episode fails the second condition. He is disappointed, not angry, and his self-report misapplies the concept. Suppose instead he half-blames the forecaster. Then the episode is admissible, though atypical. The constraint, not the word he chose, decides the case. Barrett's account explains why he reached for the word. Mine explains whether the word was correct.

**Disagreement.** Two observers watch a woman speak sharply to her manager. One calls it anger. The other calls it assertiveness. On a purely use-based account, this is a difference between two predictive histories. On mine, it is a genuine disagreement with a determinate subject matter. Did she construe the manager as accountable for a wrong? If yes, the episode is anger, however controlled. If no, it is not. The observers may lack the evidence to settle the question. But they disagree about something, and the constraint structure says what.

**Cross-cultural variation.** Now change the system. In a community whose concept of anger requires a public status violation, or whose concept merges what English separates, the admissibility conditions differ. Some episodes that English speakers call anger will not qualify there, and the reverse. This is constraint without universality. Each system binds its own instances. No system binds all of them. Translation then becomes an empirical question about overlapping constraint structures, not a search for one true anger.

**Revision.** Concepts change. Suppose this community comes to accept that anger can be directed at impersonal systems, such as "the bureaucracy," with no accountable agent. If speakers then treat such episodes as correct uses, and correct one another accordingly, they have revised the second condition. The concept has changed. That is a real event with a date and a cause. It differs from mere drift because it changes the constraint, not just the frequency of a usage. Prediction errors may motivate the revision. They do not constitute it.

**Where constraints come from.**

I now face the strongest objection to this book. My revision case says a concept changes when speakers correct one another. If the community fixes the constraints, then agreement fixes correctness after all, and my critique of Barrett rebounds on me.

Part of the objection is right. Communities do institute concepts. But instituting a standard is not applying it.

Chess shows the difference. Players instituted the rules. Once the rules stand, a move is legal or illegal whatever the onlookers believe. A whole club can call a position checkmate and be wrong. The rules decide, and the rules are not a vote on this position.

Anger works the same way. My community may have instituted condition two: anger requires an accountable agent. It does not follow that every episode the community calls anger is anger. A community can be wrong in bulk. Its own constraint convicts it. On Barrett's account, as I read it, the word and the agreement do both jobs: they group the instances and certify the grouping. Mine assigns those jobs to different layers.

Constraints are revisable, but revisable does not mean indeterminate. At any given time, a concept has a constraint structure, and applications made then answer to it. A later revision does not make earlier errors correct. It creates a new structure with a date and a cause. Philosophers of rule-following have shown how hard it is to say what fixes a rule's content from a finite record of use.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-6">6</a></sup> I do not solve that puzzle. I claim only that content and application belong on separate layers.

Constraints also answer to something other than the community. A concept serves a function. Anger registers a wrong by an accountable agent and readies a response. A structure that fired at random would fail that function by the community's own goals. Function does not pick one structure, but it rules many out.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-7">7</a></sup> And the facts push back. Whether a wrong occurred, and whether an agent construed it as one, are not up to the observers.

This changes one invariant. Goals still do not decide whether an episode is admissible, but they do judge whether a constraint structure is a good one to have.

What remains is real. I cannot show that our concept of anger is nature's. I can show that, once instituted, it stands apart from the acts that apply it. That is enough to separate error from difference.

The admissibility conditions resemble appraisal accounts of anger. Lazarus characterizes anger's core relational theme as a demeaning offense against oneself or one's own, and Frijda ties emotions to action readiness.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-8">8</a></sup> The resemblance is deliberate, but the claim differs. Appraisal theories describe a process: how an agent evaluates an encounter and how that evaluation produces the emotion. The conditions here state a constraint structure: what an episode must satisfy to count as anger within a system, however it was produced. An appraisal theorist explains how anger arises. The constraint layer says when an episode is anger. [Appendix K](../appendices/appendix-k.md) draws the line between appraisal and interpretation.

The case shows the architecture at work. Construction explains how each episode is assembled. Prediction explains how the agent anticipates and regulates. Constraint explains why the episode counts as anger rather than disappointment, assertiveness, or a successful regulatory act.

A goal is a regulatory criterion applied to admissible instances. It specifies standards of adequacy, efficiency, relevance, or compliance relative to a particular purpose. Goals regulate admissible instances after they have satisfied identity conditions; they do not determine what may count as an instance in the first place. At a higher level, goals also evaluate the constraint structure itself: they judge whether a concept serves its function well, without deciding whether any particular episode is admissible.

The architecture now contains the elements required for categorization and evaluation. Concepts fix admissibility. Instantiation realizes admissible instances. Categorization groups those instances. Goals specify the criteria by which agents evaluate them. Identity conditions determine what may count as an instance. Goal-directed evaluation determines how well categorized instances satisfy those criteria.

I can now assign categorization its proper explanatory role. Categorization groups admissible instances by similarity, typicality, and contextual relevance. It operates over determinate realizations that already satisfy conceptual constraints. It does not fix conceptual identity. It does not determine admissibility.

Goals can then regroup categorized instances from the layer above. No instance is similar in the abstract. We compare admissible instances by selecting dimensions, thresholds, and priorities that reflect those goals. The same admissible instances can support different categorizations under different goals without changing the concept.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-9">9</a></sup>

I evaluate categorized instances relative to governing criteria. Success and failure indicate how well an admissible instance satisfies the selected criterion. An instance may satisfy the concept and still fail the governing goal. Identity determines what something is; evaluation determines how well it serves a particular purpose.

I can distinguish correct from incorrect categorization only because identity conditions remain stable. A misclassification is possible only when fixed identity conditions exist against which I can compare an instance. If conceptual identity depended on successful categorization, no principled distinction between correct and incorrect grouping would remain. Disagreement would become nothing more than divergent clustering habits.

I locate correction at the level of goal-directed evaluation. When categorized instances fail to satisfy governing criteria, agents adjust execution strategies, similarity metrics, thresholds, priorities, or goal weightings rather than revising conceptual identity. Conceptual identity remains stable while its application improves.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-10">10</a></sup>

Up to this point, I have specified the architecture of conceptual identity. I now distinguish that architecture from the computational processes that operate over it. Constraint, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation define the architecture. Similarity computation, evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization execute that architecture without constituting it.

Learning refines how agents apply conceptual constraints. Agents adjust parameters, stabilize interpretive habits, and improve how they generate, select, group, and weight admissible instances relative to governing goals. They do so by responding to prediction errors, feedback, and other discrepancies between expected and observed outcomes. Those adjustments refine execution without revising conceptual identity.

Optimization likewise operates within a fixed constraint structure. Systems may update parameters, recalibrate thresholds, or refine execution strategies, but those adjustments presuppose a stable conceptual architecture. Engineers define loss functions to represent goal criteria formally. Minimizing those functions improves performance relative to specified goals without determining what the system is about. The identity of the task, the admissible mappings, and the representational role of the output must already be specified.,

No computational process generates identity conditions. Learning, optimization, evaluation, and correction all presuppose the constraint structure within which they operate. When agents respond to prediction errors by revising parameters, thresholds, or execution strategies, conceptual identity remains intact while performance improves. Only when agents revise the identity conditions themselves do they revise the concept. That distinction separates improved execution from conceptual revision.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-11">11</a></sup>

This distinction also makes the Theory of Constrained Identity compatible with computational implementation without reducing conceptual identity to statistical convergence. Learning refines execution. Optimization improves performance relative to governing goals. Neither generates the identity conditions that make those processes intelligible.

Those five computational invariants are realized in four structural layers.

Identity. Conceptual identity resides at the constraint layer as an intensional structure that fixes admissibility conditions. Every downstream process presupposes those identity conditions rather than generating them.

Instantiation. Execution occurs through admissible realizations of those identity conditions. Instantiation, embodiment, contextual realization, and implementation produce variation without revising conceptual identity.

Categorization and Goal-Directed Evaluation. Categorization organizes admissible instances according to similarity, typicality, contextual relevance, and graded membership. Goal-directed evaluation then regulates those categorized instances according to governing criteria. Neither function determines conceptual identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-12">12</a></sup>

Computational Processes. Evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization refine execution within the fixed constraint space established by the architecture. They improve execution without generating, replacing, or redefining identity conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-13">13</a></sup>

In this chapter, I have stated the Theory of Constrained Identity. I have shown that conceptual identity depends on an upstream constraint structure rather than on downstream similarity, categorization, performance, or learning. I distinguished four architectural functions—conceptual identity, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation—and separated them from the computational processes that execute them. By assigning each function its proper explanatory role, I resolved the apparent conflict between stability and variability within a single computational architecture.

From these invariants and layers follows a final restriction: no downstream success condition alone constitutes a concept. Statistical convergence, predictive accuracy, cultural uptake, and practical utility evaluate execution under constraint. Each presupposes the identity conditions it evaluates.

The Theory of Constrained Identity therefore defines the minimal computational architecture required for stability across variation, intelligible error, corrigible application, and computational implementation. Identity conditions specify admissibility. Instantiation realizes those conditions. Categorization organizes admissible instances. Goal-directed evaluation regulates them. Learning and optimization refine execution without redefining conceptual identity.,

This architecture preserves disciplined flexibility. Agents, cultures, and computational systems may apply different goals to admissible instances without multiplying concepts or revising their identity conditions. If the constraint structure changes, the concept changes. If the constraint remains and only execution varies, conceptual identity remains stable across variation.

The Theory of Constrained Identity explains the architecture of conceptual identity, but it does not yet explain how conceptual identity becomes meaning. In this chapter, I have explained how conceptual identity remains stable while execution varies, but architecture alone does not explain how concepts acquire semantic significance. Constraint structures specify what may count as an admissible instance. They do not yet explain how concepts come to stand for their referents, how signs acquire meaning, or how interpretation binds conceptual identity to reference.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-14">14</a></sup>

That is the task of Chapter 7. I now move from the architecture of conceptual identity to the architecture of meaning by developing the Constructivist Semiotics. There I explain how structured constraints become intelligible constructs and how interpretation transforms conceptual identity into semantic significance.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-15">15</a></sup>

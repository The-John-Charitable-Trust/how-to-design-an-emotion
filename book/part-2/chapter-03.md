<p align="center"><strong>CHAPTER IV</strong></p>

<p align="center"><strong>The Misappropriation of the Concept</strong></p>

<p align="center"><em>The Orphaning of Identity Conditions</em></p>

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

[Notes](../references/notes/chapter-03.md) · [Bibliography](../references/bibliography/chapter-03.md)

In Chapter 2, I established the principles that govern definition. I showed that an idea cannot survive as a slogan, a word, or a familiar example. It must have structure. It must specify the conditions under which something counts as an instance.

Chapter 3 begins with a different question. If contemporary theory rejects fixed essences, what now secures the identity conditions of concepts?

Rejecting naive essentialism does not eliminate the need for identity conditions. It relocates the problem. If concepts no longer derive their identity from essences, a theory must explain what now constrains their admissible instances. For example, we can see how some constructivist theories, particularly Barrett's Theory of Constructed Emotion, relocate conceptual stability into prediction, categorization, and use rather than reconstructing identity conditions themselves. On this approach, meaning risks becoming performance, correctness risks becoming predictive success, and disagreement risks becoming mere difference.

Without identity conditions, construction risks becoming drift.

I do not intend to restore essence. My goal is to restore conceptual constraint.

The identity condition is dead only if identity condition means essence.

Long live the identity condition as constraint.

A concept fixes what something is. It specifies the admissibility conditions under which something counts as an instance. An instance realizes those conditions under determinate values. A conceptual system organizes those admissible instances into categories, but it does not alter the admissibility conditions that govern instantiation.

Contemporary concept theory faces a structural failure that theorists rarely name. In rejecting naive essentialism, the field has not merely abandoned fixed essences. It has often abandoned identity conditions. Existing accounts explain categorization, prediction, and adaptive success, yet they struggle to explain why distinct episodes count as instances of the same emotion. These theories describe what concepts do, but they do not always secure the admissibility conditions that govern instantiation.

I address this failure through the case of Lisa Feldman Barrett's Theory of Constructed Emotion.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-1">1</a></sup> Barrett correctly rejects the classical view that emotions possess necessary and sufficient features, invariant neural signatures, or discoverable biological essences.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-2">2</a></sup> Those commitments cannot withstand empirical scrutiny. The empirical record justifies the collapse of naive essentialism.

Before I criticize Barrett's account, I state it in terms she would accept.

For Barrett, a concept is not a definition stored in the head. It is not a prototype either. In her words, "a concept is a collection of embodied, whole brain representations."<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-3">3</a></sup> The brain assembles these representations to predict what is about to happen and to prepare the body to meet it. A category, on her account, is "a population of events or objects that are treated as similar because they all serve a particular goal in some context."<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-4">4</a></sup> Concepts are therefore ad hoc in Barsalou's sense: the brain constructs them in the moment, for a goal, in a situation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-5">5</a></sup> The brain imposes similarities among diverse instances according to that goal.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-6">6</a></sup> Emotion concepts work the same way. They are goal-based concepts.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-7">7</a></sup>

Barrett does not leave these concepts adrift. Words anchor them. A shared word such as "anger" tells a child that physically different instances belong together. Communities sustain those groupings through collective agreement. Emotion concepts are real in the way money and citizenship are real: they are social reality.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-8">8</a></sup> Barrett is explicit that emotion categories are "as real as any other conceptual categories."<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-9">9</a></sup>

This is a serious account. It explains variation, context sensitivity, and cultural difference better than the classical view it replaces.

My critique answers a narrower question. I do not deny that concepts are constructed, situated, goal-based, or predictive. I ask what makes a constructed instance count as an instance of *this* concept rather than a useful prediction carrying a familiar name. Barrett's answer is the shared word and social agreement. That answer explains why a community groups instances together. It does not state the conditions an instance must satisfy for the grouping to be correct. A word labels; it does not admit. Agreement tells me what a community treats as anger. It cannot tell me when the community has misapplied its own concept, because the standard of correct application would itself be whatever the community agrees to. Social and lexical anchoring explain stability. They do not supply identity conditions. That is the gap my account fills.

Rejecting essences solves one problem but introduces another. Once essences disappear, a theory must explain what now constrains admissible instances. In discarding essences, Barrett redefines concepts operationally.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-10">10</a></sup> Barrett argues that the brain assembles concepts from prior instances for predictive purposes.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-11">11</a></sup> Stability follows performance. Persistence becomes recurrence. Meaning becomes inseparable from execution.

This move preserves flexibility and context sensitivity, but it carries a heavy cost. If a theory eliminates identity conditions rather than reconstructing them, it cannot preserve a principled distinction between correct and incorrect application. It cannot distinguish disagreement from mere difference. It redescribes error as prediction error within a regulatory model rather than as misapplication relative to specified admissibility criteria.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-12">12</a></sup> Persistence reduces to repetition. The theory explains adaptive regulation, yet it cannot explain why an episode counts as an instance rather than a revision, a replacement, or a relabeling.

My goal is not to revive essentialism. I aim to restore identity without it. Barrett's rejection of essences is correct, but her framework, as I read it, locates identity conditions in performance.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-13">13</a></sup> Concepts are neither immutable biological kinds nor momentary predictive assemblies.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-14">14</a></sup> A concept does not possess identity as an object. A concept specifies the admissibility conditions under which something counts as an instance of a given kind.

Identity attaches to instantiations relative to operative admissibility conditions. The failure I diagnose concerns the disappearance of those conditions, not the metaphysical status of concepts. I treat concepts as upstream constraint structures. These constraints fix admissible forms of application without fixing particular properties, neural realizations, or experiential uniformity.

I distinguish between identity conditions and identity. Identity conditions comprise the operative admissibility constraints a concept specifies. Identity represents the status of a particular instantiation relative to those constraints. Identity does not exist as an additional property over and above instantiation. It constitutes the normative standing of an instantiation as admissible under operative conditions.

The classical view Barrett targets assumes that each emotion possesses an internal essence. On this model, a concept consists of necessary and sufficient features that define membership.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-15">15</a></sup> This view dictates that anger has a defining structure, fear has a distinctive biological signature, and sadness carries a stable phenomenological core. If a state lacks these defining features, it fails to qualify. Identity conditions precede variation, and variation must conform to them.

Barrett challenges this framework directly. She argues that the classical view of concepts, which assumes each concept has a definition, does not survive scientific scrutiny.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-16">16</a></sup> She rejects the idea that emotions correspond to fixed biological kinds, insisting that no consistent fingerprint exists for anger or any other emotion.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-17">17</a></sup> That conclusion follows from the same empirical record. Neuroscience does not identify stable one-to-one mappings between specific brain regions and specific emotions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-18">18</a></sup> Activation patterns vary across individuals and contexts.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-19">19</a></sup> Cultural anthropology further shows variation in how emotions receive labels and meanings, while phenomenology reveals that one person's "anger" may be another person's "indignation."<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-20">20</a></sup> Naive essentialism fails because it overstates the regularity that data can support.

Barrett performs an important corrective by dismantling the expectation that emotions function like natural kinds with hidden biological substances.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-21">21</a></sup>  She shows that variability and neural reuse characterize emotional life.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-22">22</a></sup> I accept that corrective. The question remains: what secures identity conditions once essences disappear?

Barrett does not stop at rejecting essences. She replaces the classical account with an explicitly operational one.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-23">23</a></sup> In place of necessary and sufficient features, she offers a model where concepts function as dynamic resources for prediction and categorization.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-24">24</a></sup> This shift does not merely revise identity conditions. It relocates them downstream into use.

She also rejects prototype theory.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-25">25</a></sup> In her view, concepts do not exist as stored idealized averages of experience.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-26">26</a></sup> The rejection extends beyond classical essentialism to any account that treats concepts as enduring representational structures. What remains is construction in context rather than admissibility conditions that constrain instantiation.

In her foundational articles, Barrett writes that “a concept is a collection of embodied, whole brain representations” and that the brain imposes similarities between them when needed.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-27">27</a></sup> On this view, a concept does not constrain instantiation through admissibility conditions. It gathers prior experiences and groups them according to situational demands. Similarity replaces constraint.

Barrett describes concepts as tools for prediction.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-28">28</a></sup> She argues that the brain uses past instances to anticipate sensory input and regulate the body.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-29">29</a></sup> She insists that concepts are not static representations but are constructed in the moment.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-30">30</a></sup> As a result, the term concept begins to overlap with prediction and categorization themselves. Categorization does not merely apply a concept. It participates in producing the conceptual organization that then counts as meaning.

Barrett explains conceptual stability in terms of predictive success. In her account, “prediction errors are learning signals and therefore adjust subsequent predictions,” and “prediction error processing … is akin to concept learning.” On this reading, concepts persist because they serve prediction.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-31">31</a></sup> If a pattern of categorization reliably reduces uncertainty, it continues. Stability follows performance. Under this replacement strategy, a concept no longer constrains instantiation in advance. It functions as a predictive resource assembled for task success. Similarity organizes prior instances. Categorization imposes structure on sensation. Prediction regulates the body. Use determines continuity. On this reading, identity conditions become a matter of operation.

Barrett's operational account introduces a deep structural shift. Meaning no longer arises because admissibility conditions constrain application. Instead, meaning arises through the act of categorization itself.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-32">32</a></sup> She argues that the brain makes meaning of sensation by categorizing it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-33">33</a></sup> In this formulation, categorization generates intelligibility in the moment of use. When she states that concepts are constructed in the moment to meet situational demands, she identifies the act of assembling prior instances for prediction as the concept itself. Conceptual structure appears as an outcome of successful regulation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-34">34</a></sup>

My model differs in where I locate conceptual constraint. Operative admissibility conditions constrain how particular affective episodes count as instances. An episode counts as anger because it satisfies admissibility conditions operating within a conceptual system relative to an agent and a context. These conditions govern application rather than emerging from it. Categorization succeeds or fails relative to those constraints. By contrast, Barrett's model allows categorization to participate in generating conceptual organization during regulation. Stability therefore follows from regulatory success rather than from independently specified admissibility conditions.

If categorization generates meaning, then admissibility conditions cannot govern use because they emerge from it. The theory then explains persistence through the recurrence of successful assemblies rather than through operative criteria that constrain instantiation. This reversal carries heavy consequences. If conceptual structure arises from context-sensitive categorization, then nothing fixes the criteria under which distinct episodes count as instances rather than mere functional resemblances. The similarities that guide categorization vary with context and task demands, and predictive success varies with an agent's goals. Without independently articulated admissibility conditions, neither similarity nor predictive success independently determines what counts as an admissible instance. As long as regulation succeeds, conceptual stability risks becoming indistinguishable from conceptual drift.

If identity conditions are located only in use, the account owes a standard by which application counts as correct or incorrect. If a concept consists of context-sensitive prediction assembled for regulatory success, then no independent criterion governs its proper use.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-35">35</a></sup> Performance becomes the only measure.

I accept the variability Barrett describes. There is no single correct way to experience an emotion.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-36">36</a></sup> But my position does not require universal phenomenology or fixed neural signatures. Constraint does not imply universality. Identity conditions can vary across agents and still constrain instantiation within each system.

Even if an agent generates a concept at runtime, they must generate it under constraints that determine what counts as an instance. Runtime construction does not eliminate identity conditions. It presupposes them. Without constraints that distinguish anger from grief, categorization collapses into relabeling. Similarity may recur and regulation may succeed, but nothing makes an episode accountable as an instance rather than a new construction with a familiar name.

Under Barrett's framework, error reduces to failed prediction.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-37">37</a></sup> Yet predictive success does not secure identity conditions. Two different assemblies may both regulate effectively while diverging structurally. Without operative admissibility conditions, pragmatic adequacy replaces conceptual correctness.

When two agents categorize the same situation differently, Barrett's framework explains the difference by appeal to their distinct predictive histories.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-38">38</a></sup> But that explanation cannot tell us whether either agent has used the concept correctly. If no identity conditions govern the concept beyond contextual performance, then disagreement no longer concerns meaning. It becomes a difference in regulation.

The same problem appears with persistence. If concepts persist because they serve prediction, recurrence still does not secure identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-39">39</a></sup> A pattern may reappear while its boundary and structure change. Utility explains why a pattern continues. It does not explain why the same concept continues.

In this one respect, the classical view had a resource that Barrett's account must still supply. The classical view misplaced identity conditions in rigid definitions, but it preserved the idea that application answers to criteria.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-40">40</a></sup> One could misapply the concept of anger because the concept specified constraints governing its admissible instances. The classical view located those constraints in essence, but it correctly recognized that concepts require identity conditions.

Barrett escapes rigidity by locating structure in performance. The account then needs independent criteria for correct application. Once identity conditions collapse into performance, correctness becomes pragmatic success, disagreement becomes contextual divergence, and persistence becomes recurrence. I do not restore essence. I restore constraint. Instantiation must answer to operative criteria rather than to mere predictive success.

Barrett frames her project as a rejection of essences. She argues that the idea of emotional essences is a myth and that emotions lack fixed biological cores.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-41">41</a></sup> I agree with her rejection of hidden substances and invariant neural fingerprints. However, her framing creates a problem by equating identity conditions with universality. Barrett affirms that emotion categories are real, “as real as any other conceptual categories.”<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-42">42</a></sup> But her account assumes they lack stable identity conditions: nothing beyond situated use fixes what counts as an instance of anger.

The debate is often framed as a dilemma: either emotions possess discoverable essences that fix identity across all agents, or they exist only as context-sensitive constructions. The debate often appears as a choice between rigid natural kinds and radically context-sensitive construction. I propose a third architecture: constraint without universality.

I do not require universal identity conditions across all agents. I require only that instantiation answer to operative admissibility conditions for a given agent within a conceptual system. These conditions can remain agent-relative without becoming arbitrary. They can vary across systems while still functioning as constraints within each system. Barrett's critique does not appear to distinguish this alternative from the essentialism she rejects. She treats the rejection of essences as a reason to relocate conceptual stability into prediction, categorization, and use.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-43">43</a></sup>

Prototype theory fails for the same structural reason. Similarity alone cannot secure identity conditions without constraints that distinguish one concept from another. A cluster of instances does not explain what makes them instances rather than overlapping neighbors. By rejecting both essences and prototypes without separating identity from universality, Barrett does not reconstruct a layer of admissibility constraint distinct from predictive regulation.

I maintain that concepts can secure identity conditions without essences. A concept functions as a structured set of admissibility conditions within a conceptual system. These conditions determine what counts as an instance for a given agent in a given context. They do not dictate a uniform phenomenology or require cross-cultural universality. They constrain instantiation without demanding uniform realization.

I do not treat "emotion" as a single master category. Each named emotion functions as its own category composed of diverse admissible instances. Anger is not a simple instance of emotion. Anger is a structured category defined by admissibility conditions. The diversity of anger episodes does not threaten identity. It expresses it. Constraint operates at the level of each emotion, not at the level of a single genus. Identity, understood as admissibility, differs from essence in kind.

Objective structure does not require uniform substance. The Pythagorean theorem applies to infinitely many right triangles regardless of their size or material. The inputs and outputs vary, yet a governing relation persists and constrains admissible solutions. The relation remains structurally stable even though its realizations differ. The same applies here. Identity under constraint does not require identical neural patterns or cross-cultural invariance.

Neural decoding makes the same point from the other side. Each brain exhibits distinct activation patterns, yet decoding succeeds because structured mappings operate within that specific system.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-44">44</a></sup> Variation presupposes constraint.

Under my framework, anger need not possess invariant features, but once a conceptual system defines anger, its instances must satisfy the admissibility conditions that distinguish anger from grief or fear. This is an analytic truth within the system, not a claim that nature contains a universal essence of anger. These conditions may evolve, but they cannot disappear without collapsing the distinction itself. This remains compatible with construction and context sensitivity. A concept may emerge at runtime, but that assembly occurs under constraints. Construction explains instantiation. Constraint secures identity. They operate at different levels.

Identity under constraint imposes coherence rather than rigidity. It explains how an agent corrects a misclassification or recognizes two episodes as instances despite superficial differences.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-45">45</a></sup> It allows cumulative learning rather than mere repetition. By distinguishing identity conditions from essence, I preserve Barrett's empirical insights while restoring the architectural layer her framework omits.

Appendix C extends this argument through a constraint analysis of Barrett's goal-based examples. It separates identity conditions, instantiation, goal selection, runtime construction, and multi-concept classification so that goal flexibility can do its proper work without dissolving the criteria that govern admissible instances.

Barrett’s contributions regarding neural reuse and predictive processing identify features of implementation, not identity. When she shows that the same neurons create different mental states on different occasions, she demonstrates variability of realization, not an absence of constraint.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-03.md#note-46">46</a></sup> Prediction explains how episodes receive interpretation. Constraint explains what makes an episode count as an instance. The disagreement concerns where identity conditions operate. Barrett locates conceptual structure downstream in performance. I locate admissibility upstream as an operative constraint.

When identity conditions disappear, the term concept drifts between naming neural activity, predictive routines, and behavioral outcomes. This allows downstream processes to perform identity-level work without admitting the substitution. If categorization itself generates the criteria in the act of performance, then no independent structure remains by which categorization can be evaluated. Categorization cannot supply the criteria by which we normatively assess it.

I repair that problem through constraint without universality. A concept need not possess a hidden substance in nature in order to govern its instances. It must specify admissibility conditions within a conceptual system. Those conditions may be agent-relative, context-sensitive, revisable, and goal-bound, but they cannot disappear without collapsing the distinction between correct application and useful performance. Construction explains how an episode comes to be assembled. Constraint explains why that episode counts as anger rather than grief, fear, indignation, or a merely successful regulatory act.

I therefore arrive at a standard of accountability. A theory may reject essences, accept construction, and preserve context sensitivity, but it still must state the criteria by which an instance counts as what the theory calls it. Without that standard, flexibility becomes drift, and construction loses the very identity it claims to explain.

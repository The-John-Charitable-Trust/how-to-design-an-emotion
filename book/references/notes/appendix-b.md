<a id="note-2"></a>

2. On the notation, see Object Management Group, *Unified Modeling Language*. Genus and species, and identity and instantiation, are developed in chapter 3; syntagmatic ordering in chapter 2, after Ferdinand de Saussure, *Course in General Linguistics*, ed. Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye, trans. Wade Baskin (New York: Philosophical Library, 1959), pt. 2, chap. 5; the generation of episodes in chapters 2–4. The modeling claims are my own.

<a id="note-3"></a>

3. This classification is my own model; on genus and species, see chapter 3, and on the contrast with biological kinds, Appendix A.

<a id="note-4"></a>

4. On core affect as feeling good or bad, energized or enervated, see James A. Russell, “Core Affect and the Psychological Construction of Emotion,” *Psychological Review* 110, no. 1 (2003): 145–72, at 145, https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.110.1.145; treating these dimensions as primitives is my model’s choice. For accounts that favor basic affective systems, see Paul Ekman, “An Argument for Basic Emotions,” *Cognition and Emotion* 6, nos. 3–4 (1992): 169–200, at 170, https://doi.org/10.1080/02699939208411068; and Jaak Panksepp, “Cross-Species Affective Neuroscience Decoding of the Primal Affective Experiences of Humans and Related Animals,” *PLoS ONE* 6, no. 9 (2011): e21236, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0021236.

<a id="note-5"></a>

5. On moods as longer-lasting, see Ekman, “Argument for Basic Emotions,” 186; on mood as free-floating core affect and emotion as core affect attributed to something, Russell, “Core Affect,” 145. The appraisal-and-attitude constraint and the definition of DriveState are my own.

<a id="note-6"></a>

6. On definition by genus and differentiae, see Aristotle, *Topics*, trans. W. A. Pickard-Cambridge, Internet Classics Archive, https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/topics.html, I.8; on generalization hierarchies, Object Management Group, *Unified Modeling Language*, sec. 9.2.1. The mapping of the two and the identity-conditions claim are my own.

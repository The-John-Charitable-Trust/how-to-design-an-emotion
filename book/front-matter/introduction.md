# Introduction

***Rights. Morality. Emotions.***

*We live by ideas we have not yet learned how to define. We invoke rights as though their meaning were self-evident. We appeal to morality as though its authority were unquestionable. We speak of emotions as though everyone already knows what they are. Yet beneath this confidence lies a deeper problem: many of our most important mind-dependent concepts rest upon inherited definitions, unstable intuitions, and assumptions that have rarely been subjected to disciplined examination.*

*This is not merely a book about emotion. It is a book about the architecture and construction of meaning across domains. Emotion serves as the proof of concept because it exposes the problem at its most immediate and personal. We feel before we explain. We react before we interpret. We name our inner states before we understand the conditions that make those names valid.*

*To confront that problem, I proceed through what I call a **metamorphosis of ontology**. I begin with what first appears in experience: affect, feeling, and ordinary emotional language. I then progressively refine those appearances into increasingly precise identity conditions until the inquiry reaches a mature ontology of affect, feeling, emotion, and value.*

*The book develops **five complementary frameworks**: the **Goal-Governed Constraint Model of Concepts**, **Constructivist Semiotics**, the **Constructivist Semiotic Model of Emotions**, the **Constructivist Interpretation Theory of Emotion**, and the **Computational Model of Emotions**. Each framework answers a question made unavoidable by the previous one: how concepts preserve identity, how signs become meaning, how affect becomes emotion, where appraisal ends and interpretation begins, and how these structures survive formal representation and computational implementation.*

*I also refuse to address this problem from within the boundaries of a single discipline. Philosophy clarifies identity. Affective neuroscience establishes the biological foundation. Cognitive psychology explains the working mind. Semiotics explains symbolic meaning. Music theory models qualitative variation. Computer science tests formal coherence. Each discipline reveals an indispensable part of the whole. None, by itself, explains the complete architecture of emotional meaning.*

*The question, therefore, is not simply what an emotion is. The deeper question is how the mind transforms raw experience into structured meaning without collapsing into reductionism, relativism, or conceptual indeterminacy. If we cannot answer that question, we cannot fully understand what we feel, what we value, what we owe one another, or what kind of beings we are.*

*The task is not to inherit meaning, but to make it conscious. Define the ideas that govern human life. Test them. Discipline them. Then live by them.*

The protection of rights, the guidance of morality, and the intelligibility of emotion are not peripheral concerns of civilization. They help make civilized life possible. Yet we often fail to identify the governing principles that give them coherence. I treat these abstractions as mind-dependent concepts, not as objects that arise ready-made from the world. The mind must define, interpret, and validate them according to objective standards. Without such standards, concepts lose the identity conditions by which we apply, defend, correct, and understand them.¹

When we neglect this responsibility, we mistake familiarity for knowledge. When we accept inherited definitions as self-evident truths, we expose the foundations of human meaning to instability, fragmentation, and collapse. Rights require a principled account of the values they protect.² Morality requires a defensible account of obligation.³ Emotion requires an ontology capable of distinguishing bodily affect, conscious feeling, and interpreted meaning.⁴ I deliberately invert the biblical image:

*The stone that the builders accepted has become the chief cornerstone.*⁵

By this I mean that thinkers, institutions, and architects of contemporary thought have accepted certain definitions as given and then built entire systems upon foundations they never validated. The builders of modern ideas often assume coherence while leaving the rules that sustain it undefined. I begin from the opposite premise: the integrity of our ideas depends upon the principles by which we form, govern, and validate them.

Consider first the example of rights. Jefferson drew upon Locke's language of natural rights, but the Declaration of Independence transformed that inheritance by grounding political legitimacy in self-evident truths, unalienable rights, and government by consent.⁶

In my account, the self-evident and unalienable truths of the Declaration are not themselves rights. They are the values that rights exist to protect.

I do not treat rights as free-floating moral slogans. I treat them as normative authorities that protect underlying values and impose correlative obligations. Hohfeld's analysis clarifies this point: every claim-right entails a corresponding duty in another party.⁷ When political traditions detach rights from the values and obligations that justify them, rights lose conceptual discipline and become rhetorical absolutes.

Natural law binds because God commands through the rational order of nature.⁸ Jurisdictional law binds because government mandates it.⁹ But by what authority does morality bind?

If I cannot answer that question, moral discourse collapses into sentiment, preference, social pressure, or political power.

Emotion theory faces the same architectural failure. Many contemporary approaches in the life sciences explain emotion through physiology, appraisal, prediction, expression, or social construction, yet often leave the identity conditions of emotion unstable.¹⁰ I do not deny the reality of affect, feeling, expression, or bodily activation. I deny that any one of them, taken alone, provides an adequate ontology of emotion. A theory of emotion must explain how bodily affect becomes conscious feeling, how feeling receives interpretation, and how interpretation becomes emotional meaning.

Many contemporary approaches in the life sciences treat emotions as natural kinds located primarily in biological processes. They often conflate bodily activation with meaningful interpretation, attempting to explain emotional experience before explaining how biological affect acquires cognitive and symbolic significance. This confusion now extends into the growing emotion-detection technology industry, where systems claim to identify emotions from facial, vocal, or physiological signals before establishing the identity conditions that make such identification valid.¹¹

Examining emotion therefore exposes the same architectural problem that appears in rights and morality. A mind-dependent concept cannot survive on examples, measurements, intuitions, or inherited categories alone. It requires principles that identify what the concept must preserve across contexts, applications, and interpretations. I narrow the book's focus to the ontology of emotion because emotion provides the decisive test case for the broader problem of how the mind constructs, stabilizes, and governs meaning.

Are emotions innate biological responses triggered directly by the world, or does the mind shape them through interpretation? Do external events cause emotions, or does a conscious agent organize raw affect into meaning? Do animals and infants have emotions, or do they undergo affect and feeling without reaching symbolic and interpretive emotional understanding?¹²

What exactly is an emotion, and how does it differ from sensation, physiological response, conscious feeling, appraisal, or judgment?

These questions do not merely concern emotion theory. They concern the conditions under which any subjective experience becomes intelligible. To define emotion, I must do more than describe what people feel. I must identify the architecture that organizes feeling into meaning.

**Section 2**

I do not begin with a fixed definition. I begin with a method. This book depends upon a **progressive ontology**: a disciplined movement from preliminary description toward increasingly precise identity conditions. Aristotle distinguished nominal accounts from deeper definitions that disclose what a thing is. Kant distinguished raw appearance from the structured conditions under which the mind can know an object.¹³ I draw from both movements without reducing my method to either one. I begin with what first appears: affect, feeling, and ordinary emotional language. I then progressively refine those appearances into an ontology capable of explaining what emotion must be.

I proceed chronologically and methodically. First, I examine **physiological affect**: the bodily and neural processes that generate arousal, valence, tension, attraction, aversion, activation, and regulation. Second, I examine **first-person feeling**: the conscious awareness of affect as lived experience. Third, I examine **cognitive-semiotic emotion**: the interpretive act through which a conscious agent organizes feeling under concepts, symbols, goals, and evaluative meaning.

I do not discard earlier truths as the inquiry advances. I preserve them by reorganizing them within a more comprehensive ontology. Affect remains real. Feeling remains real. Interpretation remains real. The task is not to choose one level and abolish the others, but to identify the conditions that distinguish them, the hierarchy that orders them, and the architecture that integrates them into a coherent account of emotional meaning.

This book confronts emotional meaning without reducing it to biology or dissolving it into culture. Emotional meaning emerges through the interaction of biological affect, cognitive processing, symbolic interpretation, and rational evaluation. I therefore cannot solve the problem from within one discipline alone. I draw from philosophy, affective neuroscience, cognitive psychology, semiotics, music theory, and computer science because each field reveals one necessary condition of the whole.

Philosophy gives me the tools to ask what an emotion is, what identity conditions it must preserve, what makes an emotional judgment correct or mistaken, and how a conscious agent evaluates internal experience. Empirical observation can describe bodily activity, behavior, or reportable feeling, but it cannot by itself explain how those phenomena acquire meaning for a mind.<sup>14</sup>

Affective neuroscience grounds the inquiry in biological reality. The body supplies arousal, valence, regulation, allostasis, and neural constraint. I do not treat emotion as a disembodied act of interpretation. I treat bodily affect as the indispensable material from which feeling and emotional meaning later arise.<sup>15</sup>

Cognitive psychology explains how the mind filters perception, directs attention, retrieves memory, manages cognitive load, and coordinates goal-directed processing. Without this perspective, the theory would risk becoming a static formalism. Emotional meaning arises in a working mind that selects, prioritizes, compares, and organizes experience under pressure.<sup>16</sup>

Semiotics explains how private experience becomes communicable meaning. A feeling locked inside the body remains a private occurrence. To become emotional meaning, it must enter a symbolic order. It must become something an agent can name, interpret, compare, revise, and share. Semiotics gives me the bridge between internal experience and public intelligibility.<sup>17</sup>

Music theory gives me a model for emotional variation. Many affective theories reduce feeling to broad categories such as pleasure and displeasure, but emotional experience has texture, contour, intensity, rhythm, and qualitative color. I draw on affective timbre and emotional genre to explain how emotional categories preserve identity while allowing rich variation across instances.<sup>18</sup>

Computer science gives me the final test of architectural discipline. If a theory claims to define concepts, signs, emotions, and interpretive rules, then I must formalize its structure, model its operations, and inspect its logic. I do not claim that truth reduces to computation. I claim that a theory of meaning gains discipline when its definitions can survive formal representation, UML architecture, simulation, and executable constraint.<sup>19</sup>

Together, these disciplines let me pursue a unified ontology without collapsing the levels of explanation. Philosophy clarifies identity. Neuroscience supplies affective reality. Psychology explains cognitive processing. Semiotics explains symbolic meaning. Music theory models qualitative variation. Computer science tests formal coherence. The book requires all of them because emotional meaning requires all of them.

These disciplines do not appear as parallel commentaries. They function as complementary levels of explanation within a unified ontology. Together they reveal four linked problems, each requiring a corresponding theoretical framework.

These disciplines do not appear as parallel commentaries. They function as complementary levels of explanation within a unified ontology. Together they reveal **seven** interrelated problems, each requiring a corresponding theoretical framework.

The first problem is conceptual: **how can a concept preserve identity while allowing variation across cases, contexts, and goals?** This problem requires the **Theory of Constrained Identity**. Chapter 1, *Why We Don’t Agree on the Definition of Emotion: What It Takes to Get It Right*, establishes the definitional crisis in emotion theory. Chapter 2, *The Fragility of Belief: A Cognitive and Computational Approach for Defining Ideas*, asks how the mind defines an idea, while Appendices A and B introduce foundational emotion theory and the formal UML architecture that motivates the reconstruction. Chapter 3, *Beyond Naive Essentialism*: *Reconstructing Identity Conditions*, confronts the collapse of naïve essentialism without abandoning identity conditions. Chapter 4, *The Misappropriation of the Concept: The Orphaning of Identity Conditions*, examines the confusion between identity and categorization, supported by Appendices C and D. Chapter 5, *Classical vs Prototype Theories of Concepts: A Faux Dichotomy*, resolves the apparent opposition between classical and prototype theories, with Appendix E extending the analysis into layered ontology. Chapter 6, *Structure vs Variability: A Goal-Governed Model of Conceptual Identity*, brings the conceptual problem to its formal resolution.

The second problem is semiotic: **how does structured meaning become possible for an interpreting mind?** This problem requires **Constructivist Semiotics**. Chapter 7, *The Constructivist Semiotics: The Architecture of Meaning*, establishes the architecture of meaning itself. Chapter 8, *The War of Signs: Saussure, Peirce and the Reconstruction of Meaning*, evaluates whether the dominant semiotic traditions can preserve identity, reference, interpretation, and agency under formal constraint.

The third problem is emotional: how does affect become emotional meaning? This problem requires the Constructivist Semiotic Model of Emotions. Chapter 9, *Emotions Are Not Triggered, They Are Made: A Constructivist Semiotic Model of Emotions*, develops the central thesis that agents construct emotional meaning by organizing affective experience under symbolic and interpretive constraint rather than by triggering finished biological objects. Appendix F establishes the candidate theories, frameworks, and models that enter the comparative trade study. Appendix G justifies the comparative methodology and weighting rationale. Appendix H explains the macro, and micro-level weighting of the governing domains and principles. Appendix I applies that methodology by presenting the detailed scoring rationales and consolidated weighted score matrix that identify the preserved architectural contributions to the Constructivist Semiotic Model of Emotions.

The fourth problem is computational: **can emotional construction be represented as a formal computational architecture?** This problem requires the **Constructivist Computational Model of Emotions**. Appendix J, *Constructivist Computational Model of Emotions: A Hypothetical Architecture*, develops the computational realization of the preceding theory and demonstrates how emotional construction can be represented within a formal symbolic system.

The fifth problem is interpretive: **where does appraisal end and interpretation begin?** This problem requires the **Constructivist Interpretation Theory of Emotion**. Appendix K, *The Constructivist Interpretation Theory of Emotion: Where Appraisal Ends and Interpretation Begins*, distinguishes appraisal from interpretation and argues that appraisal alone cannot account for emotional meaning because interpretation introduces symbolic, conceptual, and evaluative organization.

The sixth problem is architectural: **how do these explanatory components become a unified theory of emotion?** This problem requires **Emotional Constructivism**. Appendix L, *Emotional Constructivism: A Formal Architecture of Emotion as Interpretive Sign*, integrates the semiotic, computational, and interpretive frameworks into a unified formal architecture of emotional meaning.

The seventh problem is linguistic: **how does emotional meaning function as a symbolic language?** This problem requires the **Constructivist Linguistics of Emotion**. Chapter 10, *All I Know Is What I Have Symbols For: The Making of Meaning*, establishes the semantic foundations of emotional meaning, supported by Appendix M, *Formal CSS Walkthrough: The Color Red, The Sound of a Tree and Gamelan*. Chapter 11, *Do Animals and Infants Have Emotions?: Symbolic Thresholds and the Emergence of Emotional Meaning*, identifies the symbolic threshold required for emotional competence. Chapter 12, *Our Feelings Are Real, But Are Our Emotions?: The Misattribution of Affect*, distinguishes affect, feeling, physiology, and emotional interpretation. Chapter 13, *The Sound of Feelings: Music as a Model for Affective Mechanisms, Felt Timbre, and Emotion Genres*, develops the grammar of emotional meaning through the analogy of music, while Appendix N, *Mathematical Realism and Emotional Grammar: From Ontology of Structure to Ontology of Interpretation*, extends the framework into emotional grammar and formal interpretation.

These seven frameworks progressively reconstruct the architecture of emotional meaning. Together they establish the conceptual, semiotic, computational, interpretive, and linguistic foundations required for a unified account of emotion.

The book culminates in Chapter 14, *A Unified Ontology of Value: The Intersection of Ethics and Emotions*. Rather than introducing another framework, the final chapter integrates the preceding ones into a single ontological account of affect, feeling, emotion, and value. It asks how these domains relate without collapsing into one another and offers the book's final synthesis of meaning, emotion, and ethical evaluation.

The map now returns to the governing failure: dominant approaches in affective science often confuse the reality of affect with the identity of emotion. Bodily activation exists. Facial expression exists. Conscious feeling exists. I do not deny any of them. I deny that any one of them, taken alone, gives us emotion. When a theory treats physiology, expression, appraisal, prediction, or linguistic convention as the whole of emotion, it mistakes one explanatory level for the entire architecture.²⁰

This architectural confusion becomes most visible in emotion-detection technologies, animal consciousness, infant development, and affective neuroscience. In each of these domains, researchers often treat observable reactions as evidence of fully formed emotions. Yet observable evidence underdetermines emotional meaning. A reaction does not yet give us meaning. A facial movement does not yet give us interpretation. A physiological signal does not yet give us emotional identity. Before I can say that an organism has an emotion, I must first know whether it possesses the symbolic, interpretive, and evaluative capacities required to organize affective experience into emotional meaning.²¹

A theory of emotion must therefore restore the rational agent to the center of the problem. It must explain how an agent constructs, evaluates, and revises emotional meaning. It must distinguish automatic affective signals from conceptually organized emotional judgments. It must also explain how an agent recognizes and corrects errors in emotional interpretation. Without these mechanisms, emotion theory continues to confuse affective signal with emotional meaning.²²

I do not leave this inquiry inside the academy. I use my novel, *God Father*, as a narrative vehicle because fiction can dramatize philosophical, psychological, ethical, and emotional conflict in a form readers can inhabit. The novel does not replace the theoretical framework. It gives the framework a human stage.

Alongside the book and the novel, I will bring these ideas into the wider world through videos, podcast episodes, glossary entries, and a conversational AI avatar. I want readers to encounter this architecture not as an abstraction sealed inside academic discourse, but as a living framework for understanding rights, morality, emotion, and meaning in ordinary life.

That wider task returns us to the central question of the book: before I define what an emotion is, I must clarify what an ontology of emotion must preserve. I do not treat emotional meaning as something we merely inherit from biology, culture, or language. I treat it as something the mind constructs, stabilizes, revises, and inhabits. This is why consensus on the definition of emotion remains so elusive, and why Chapter 1 begins with the deeper problem: why we do not agree on the definition of emotion, and what it would take to get it right.
